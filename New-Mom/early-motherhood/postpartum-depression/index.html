Postpartum Depression: You Deserve Support, Too

Becoming a mother can bring love, change, uncertainty, and exhaustion. But if sadness, emptiness, anxiety, or hopelessness feels persistent or overwhelming, it may be more than the adjustment you expected.

Postpartum depression is a recognised mental health condition. It is not a personal failure, a lack of love for your baby, or a sign that you are not a good mother. Support and treatment are available.

What is postpartum depression?

Postpartum depression is depression that occurs after childbirth. It can affect mothers in different ways and may begin in the weeks after birth or later during the first year.

The experience is not the same for everyone. Some mothers feel persistently sad, while others feel numb, disconnected, unusually irritable, anxious, or unable to enjoy activities that once mattered to them.

Postpartum depression can affect sleep, appetite, concentration, relationships, daily activities, and feelings about oneself. Some symptoms may overlap with the normal demands of caring for a newborn, which is one reason professional assessment can help.

You do not have to wait until you are unable to function before asking for help.

Possible signs and symptoms

Symptoms can vary, but may include:

  • Persistent sadness, emptiness, or frequent crying.
  • Loss of interest or enjoyment in things you previously liked.
  • Feeling hopeless, worthless, guilty, or like you are failing.
  • Strong irritability, restlessness, or emotional overwhelm.
  • Difficulty concentrating or making everyday decisions.
  • Changes in appetite or sleep beyond what you would expect from baby care.
  • Feeling distant or disconnected from others.
  • Finding daily responsibilities unusually difficult to manage.
  • Persistent worry or frightening thoughts that distress you.

These examples are not a self-diagnosis checklist. Experiencing one symptom does not automatically mean you have postpartum depression. A qualified healthcare professional can assess your symptoms and help identify the support you need.

Postpartum depression and the baby blues: What is the difference?

The baby blues are common emotional changes that often occur during the first days after childbirth. They may include tearfulness, sensitivity, mood changes, and feeling overwhelmed. These feelings generally improve within about two weeks.

Postpartum depression can be more intense, last longer, or interfere more significantly with everyday life. It can also develop after the early newborn period.

There is no need to wait for two weeks if symptoms are severe, worsening, or frightening. Seek professional advice whenever you are concerned.

Why can postpartum depression happen?

There is no single cause. Postpartum depression may involve a combination of biological, emotional, social, and environmental factors.

  • Physical and hormonal changes after childbirth.
  • Sleep disruption and exhaustion.
  • A previous history of depression, anxiety, or other mental health difficulties.
  • A difficult pregnancy, birth, or recovery experience.
  • Limited practical or emotional support.
  • Relationship stress, financial pressure, or major life changes.
  • Feeling overwhelmed by expectations about motherhood.

Postpartum depression can affect someone who has a supportive family, a wanted pregnancy, and a healthy baby. It does not mean that the mother caused it or could have prevented it by trying harder.

How to ask for help

Starting a conversation can feel difficult, especially if you worry that others will misunderstand you. You do not have to explain everything perfectly.

You could start by telling a trusted person:

"I haven't been feeling like myself. I'm struggling emotionally, and I need support. Can you help me speak with a healthcare professional?"

You can contact your obstetrician, primary care clinician, psychiatrist, psychologist, or another qualified healthcare professional. Tell them when your symptoms began, how they affect your day, and whether you have experienced similar difficulties before.

If speaking feels difficult, write down your concerns or ask someone you trust to accompany you to an appointment.

What kinds of support may help?

Appropriate support depends on your symptoms, medical history, preferences, and circumstances. A healthcare professional can discuss options with you.

Psychological support

Evidence-based talking therapies can help people understand difficult feelings, develop coping strategies, and work through depression-related patterns of thinking.

Medical treatment

Medication may be recommended in some circumstances. If you are breastfeeding or expressing milk, tell your clinician so they can discuss the benefits, risks, and suitable options for your situation. Do not start, stop, or change prescribed medication without professional guidance.

Practical help at home

Help with meals, household responsibilities, appointments, and baby care may reduce some of the daily pressure while you receive treatment.

Ongoing follow-up

Recovery can take time. Follow-up appointments give you and your healthcare professional an opportunity to review how you are feeling and adjust your support when necessary.

How partners and family can support a new mother

Loved ones do not need to have all the answers. Their presence, patience, and practical help can make it easier for a mother to seek and continue care.

  • Listen without blaming, dismissing, or comparing her experience.
  • Take concerns seriously, even if she appears fine at other times.
  • Help with meals, appointments, household tasks, and baby care.
  • Encourage professional support without forcing her to hide her feelings.
  • Check in regularly rather than assuming she will ask for help.
  • Offer to accompany her to an appointment if she wants that support.

Avoid statements such as "You should be happy" or "Just think positively." Compassion and practical help are more useful than pressure to feel differently.

When to seek urgent help

Please take these warning signs seriously

Seek urgent professional or emergency help if you have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, feel unable to keep yourself or your baby safe, or experience severe confusion, hallucinations, delusions, or a sudden loss of contact with reality.

These symptoms may indicate a psychiatric emergency. Do not wait for a routine appointment. If there is an immediate safety concern, ask a trusted adult to stay with you and contact local emergency services.

In India, call 112 for immediate emergency assistance when required.

If you are worried about your mental health but are not in immediate danger, arrange a prompt appointment with a qualified healthcare professional.

Recovery is possible, and you deserve care

Postpartum depression can make it difficult to imagine feeling better. With appropriate support and treatment, many people improve and find ways to manage their symptoms.

Recovery does not have to mean doing everything alone, returning to your old routine immediately, or pretending that the difficult days never happened. It means getting the support you need and taking things one step at a time.

You are still you. You deserve understanding, respect, and professional care just as much as anyone else.

Asking for help does not make you less of a mother. It is one way of caring for yourself and your family.
