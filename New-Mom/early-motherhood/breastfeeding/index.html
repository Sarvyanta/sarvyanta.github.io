Breastfeeding: A Gentle Starting Guide for New Moms

Breastfeeding can be a learning experience for both you and your baby. There may be tender moments, questions, unexpected challenges, and days when you wonder whether you are doing it right.

You do not need to know everything from the beginning. Learning your baby's cues, getting practical support, and asking questions can help you find an approach that works for your family.

Every breastfeeding journey is different

Some babies learn to latch quickly, while others need more time and support. Feeding can also be affected by birth experiences, the baby's health, milk supply, pain, family circumstances, and the mother's recovery.

You may hear many opinions from relatives, friends, and social media. Remember that your baby's needs and your health matter more than meeting someone else's expectations.

Breastfeeding support should help you feel informed and respected, not judged. If breastfeeding is difficult, you deserve help rather than blame.

1. Learn to notice your baby's early feeding cues

Babies often show signs of hunger before they begin crying. Depending on their age and behaviour, these may include:

  • Moving their head and opening their mouth to search for the breast.
  • Bringing their hands toward their mouth.
  • Making sucking movements or sounds.
  • Becoming more alert or restless.

Crying can be a later sign of hunger, and a crying baby may need calming before feeding is comfortable. These cues are useful guides, but every baby is different.

Follow your baby's feeding plan if a healthcare professional has recommended one, especially if your baby was premature, is unwell, has jaundice, or has concerns about weight gain.

2. Find a comfortable position

Feeding may take time, so support your back, shoulders, and arms. Use pillows for your comfort if helpful, while keeping pillows and other soft items away from your baby's sleep space.

A lactation consultant or trained healthcare professional can demonstrate positions such as cradle, cross-cradle, football hold, or side-lying feeding where appropriate.

A few things to consider during a feed:

  • Bring your baby toward your body rather than leaning forward for the whole feed.
  • Keep your baby's head and body supported and aligned.
  • Allow your baby to approach the breast with their mouth open widely.
  • If feeding is persistently painful, ask someone qualified to assess the latch.

Positioning advice may need to be adapted for your recovery, your baby's age, and any medical considerations.

3. What does a comfortable latch feel like?

Some initial sensitivity may occur, but ongoing pain, damaged nipples, or repeated difficulty getting your baby to latch should not be ignored.

During effective feeding, you may notice rhythmic sucking and swallowing. Your baby's cheeks generally remain rounded rather than pulling inward, and feeding should become more comfortable once the latch is established.

No single sign proves that your baby is receiving enough milk. Your baby's weight trend, feeding behaviour, and wet and dirty nappies are important parts of the overall picture.

If you are unsure, ask a healthcare professional or lactation consultant to observe a feed and help assess your baby's intake.

4. How often should a baby breastfeed?

Newborns commonly feed frequently, sometimes in clusters with shorter intervals between feeds. Feeding patterns can change as babies grow, and a baby's needs may differ from one day to another.

Rather than relying on a rigid schedule for every baby, follow hunger cues and the individual feeding advice you have received. Some newborns need to be woken for feeds, particularly when there are concerns about weight, jaundice, prematurity, or illness.

If your baby is unusually sleepy, difficult to wake for feeds, feeding poorly, or producing fewer wet nappies than expected, contact your baby's healthcare professional promptly.

5. Understanding milk supply concerns

Many new mothers worry that they do not have enough milk, especially when a baby feeds often or wants to feed again soon after finishing.

Frequent feeding alone does not necessarily mean that milk supply is low. However, it is important not to assume that everything is fine without checking how your baby is feeding and growing.

A healthcare professional can help review:

  • Your baby's weight gain and growth pattern.
  • Feeding effectiveness and latch.
  • Wet nappies and other age-appropriate signs of intake.
  • Any health conditions or circumstances that could affect feeding.

If additional feeding is recommended, ask how to meet your baby's needs while supporting your own feeding goals. Supplementing with expressed milk or infant formula may be appropriate in some circumstances; individual advice is best.

6. Breastfeeding, expressing milk, and returning to work

Some parents breastfeed directly, some express milk, and others combine breastfeeding with formula feeding. The right approach depends on your baby's needs, your circumstances, and your preferences.

If you plan to express milk, a lactation professional can help with pump fit, technique, and a schedule that suits your situation. Follow reliable guidance for storing, transporting, and preparing expressed milk.

If you are returning to work, consider discussing feeding breaks, a private place to express milk, storage arrangements, and childcare routines ahead of time.

You do not have to make every decision immediately. Ask questions and make a plan that is practical for your family.

7. Look after yourself while feeding your baby

Feeding a baby can take considerable time and energy. Try to keep water and easy meals available, rest when support makes it possible, and ask someone to help with household tasks.

You do not need a special diet to be a good mother. If you have dietary restrictions, medical conditions, concerns about medicines, or questions about supplements, discuss them with a qualified healthcare professional.

Pain, exhaustion, or emotional distress deserve attention too. Your comfort is part of caring for your baby.

When should you seek medical advice?

Contact a qualified healthcare professional if your baby is feeding poorly, is unusually sleepy or difficult to wake, has signs of dehydration, or is not gaining weight as expected. Newborns can become unwell quickly, so do not delay when you are concerned.

Seek prompt medical advice if you develop breast redness, worsening pain, swelling, fever, or flu-like symptoms. These may need assessment rather than self-treatment alone.

Seek urgent medical care if your baby has difficulty breathing, turns blue or grey, is unresponsive, or appears seriously unwell. Use local emergency services when needed.

You deserve support, not pressure

If feeding is painful, stressful, or not going as expected, reach out to your baby's healthcare professional or a qualified lactation consultant. Asking for help early can make it easier to understand your options.

If breastfeeding is not going the way you imagined

You may have planned to breastfeed exclusively and discovered that it is more difficult than you expected. You may need temporary supplementation, ongoing support, or a different feeding plan.

It is okay to feel disappointed, relieved, uncertain, or several emotions at once. Your feeding journey does not determine your worth as a mother.

The priority is a baby who receives appropriate nutrition and a parent who is supported, informed, and treated with compassion. You can ask questions, review your options, and make decisions with qualified support.

You are learning your baby, and your baby is learning you. You do not have to figure it all out alone.
