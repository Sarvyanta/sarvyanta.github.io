THE FIRST DAYS TOGETHER

Coming Home with Your Baby

Bringing your baby home can be a moment you have imagined for months. It can also feel surprisingly unfamiliar. The hospital support is behind you, new routines are beginning, and you are learning how to care for your baby while recovering yourself.

SETTLING INTO A NEW RHYTHM

It is normal for home to feel different

Before your baby arrives, you may imagine how the first days will look. The reality can be quieter than expected, busier than expected or simply different. Feeding, changing, soothing and sleeping may happen in a repeating cycle, while your own recovery and emotions need attention too.

You do not need to create a perfect routine immediately. It is enough to focus on the basics, learn your baby's cues gradually and ask questions when you are unsure.

You are not expected to know everything on the first day at home.

Keep the first days simple

If possible, reduce non-essential commitments. Visitors, household tasks and messages can wait when you need rest or quiet time. You can decide what feels comfortable for you and your baby.

  • Keep essential baby supplies in a convenient place.
  • Follow the feeding and care guidance given by your healthcare team.
  • Keep contact details for your maternity team and baby's healthcare provider accessible.
  • Accept practical help with meals, groceries or household tasks.
  • Give yourself time to recover and learn without comparing your experience with others.

Let people know what would help

People may offer to visit or hold the baby, but you might need something different. You may need a meal, help with washing, a lift to an appointment or someone to listen while you talk.

It is reasonable to be specific about what would make your day easier. You can also set limits on visitors and ask them to postpone a visit if you need rest.

Make room for your recovery

Recovery after birth varies from person to person and depends on your health and birth experience. Follow your healthcare professional's instructions and attend recommended follow-up appointments. Ask for advice if you are unsure whether a symptom is expected.

Seek urgent medical care for severe or concerning symptoms, including heavy bleeding, chest pain, difficulty breathing, fainting, severe headache or thoughts of harming yourself or your baby. Do not wait for an online article to tell you what to do.

You are learning your baby, too

Newborns communicate through cues that can take time to recognise. You may not understand every cry immediately. Learning happens through observation, support and time; it is not a test you must pass on your first attempt.

A gentle reminder: You do not have to manage every task or answer every message. Rest, recovery and support belong in these first days too.
