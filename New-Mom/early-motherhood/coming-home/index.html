The first days

Coming Home With Your Baby

Bringing your baby home can feel joyful, strange, tender and overwhelming—all at once. You do not have to make these first days look a certain way.

Let the First Days Be Simple

You may have imagined what the first days with your baby would look like. The reality may be calmer than expected, more demanding than expected, or a mixture of both. Whatever your experience, you do not need to follow someone else's version of new motherhood.

Focus on the essentials: feeding your baby, resting when you can, staying hydrated and learning one another's rhythms. Laundry, messages and non-essential chores can wait.

Some days, a shower and a meal may feel like major accomplishments. It is okay if your routine is different from what it used to be.

Make Space for Your Recovery

Your body needs time to recover after birth. Recovery differs from person to person and may depend on the type of birth, any complications and your individual health.

Follow the discharge instructions given by your maternity team and attend your recommended follow-up appointments. If you are unsure whether a symptom is expected, contact your healthcare professional rather than guessing.

If possible, keep water, easy snacks, your phone and feeding supplies within reach. Ask someone you trust to help with meals, laundry, shopping or other everyday tasks.

Accepting help does not mean you are not coping. It is one way to protect your recovery and give yourself time to adjust.

You Can Set Boundaries With Visitors

Friends and family may be excited to meet your baby. Their excitement can be lovely, but you are not required to host visitors before you feel ready.

You can ask people to postpone a visit, keep it short, wash their hands, or avoid visiting when they are unwell. It is also okay to ask people not to kiss your newborn.

If you need words to explain your boundary, try:

"We are settling in and resting right now. We will let you know when we are ready for visitors. Thank you for understanding."

A helpful visit should support your family, not create more work for you. Someone might bring a meal, tidy the kitchen or help with errands instead of expecting you to entertain them.

Learning Your Baby Takes Time

You and your baby are getting to know each other. Feeding, soothing, sleep and daily routines may take time to understand. You do not need to know every answer on the first day.

Ask your baby's healthcare professional about feeding, nappies, weight checks, jaundice, sleep and any other concerns you have. It is better to ask a question than to feel that you must work everything out alone.

Follow current safe-sleep advice from your healthcare team. In general, babies should be placed on their backs on a firm, flat, separate sleep surface, without loose bedding or soft objects.

Your Feelings Matter, Too

You might feel deeply connected to your baby immediately. You might feel uncertain, numb, tearful or surprised by how much your life has changed. Feelings can also change from hour to hour.

There is no need to compare your experience with another parent's story. If your emotions feel intense, persistent or difficult to manage, tell someone you trust and speak with a qualified healthcare professional.

You deserve care as a person, not only as a caregiver.

When to Seek Medical Help

Contact your maternity team or healthcare professional if you are worried about your physical recovery, your baby's feeding, your baby's behaviour or your emotional wellbeing.

Seek urgent medical care for symptoms such as heavy bleeding, chest pain, difficulty breathing, fainting, severe or persistent headache, vision changes or severe abdominal pain after birth.

For your baby, seek prompt medical advice if your baby is difficult to wake, has trouble breathing, feeds poorly, appears dehydrated or develops a fever. A fever in a very young infant needs prompt medical assessment.

If you have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, hallucinations, severe confusion or feel detached from reality, seek emergency professional help immediately. If possible, stay with a trusted adult while help is arranged.

A gentle reminder

This article provides general information and emotional support. It does not diagnose, treat or replace advice from a qualified healthcare professional.
